DON'T PANIC

Tying It All Together, Explained

Start here: everything you own is held up by string

Look around the aftermath. The tarp over your head is tied. The load on the cart is strapped. The water bucket hangs from a knot somebody tied, once, and trusted. Civilization, it turns out, was held together with rope and knots the whole time — the grid just hid it from you.

This book assumes you have never tied anything more serious than your shoes. That's fine. Every knot here is shown big, in steps, with the one job it's for and the one job it must never be given. Tie each one ten times on the couch and it's yours forever — knots are the cheapest skill in this whole series: the practice material is string.

THE ONE RULE: test every tie like it wants to fail, because one day it will try. Pull it hard, bounce the load, wiggle it, then look at it again. A knot you haven't loaded is a rumor. And no rope, strap, or knot in this book is for holding a person — not climbing, not lowering, not swinging. People get the rated gear or they stay on the ground.

1. Four different animals: rope, cord, webbing, bungee

People say "rope" for four things that behave completely differently. Using the wrong one is the beginner's classic mistake:

  • Rope — the round, thick one, a finger's width and up. Twisted or braided fibers. The general-purpose muscle: hauling, tying loads, hanging things.
  • Cord — rope's thin little sibling: paracord, clothesline, twine, shoelaces. For light jobs — tarps, bundles, repairs — and for practicing knots. Paracord (the parachute stuff, about 4mm) is the aftermath's duct tape: carry some everywhere.
  • Webbing / straps — flat woven bands, like seatbelts and ratchet straps. Flat spreads the load so it doesn't cut into things, and it doesn't roll or stretch much. This is what holds cargo down.
  • Bungee cord — a rubber core in a cloth jacket, with hooks. It is a spring, not a rope. It holds things snug, never secure, and it stores energy that it would love to give back to your face. Entry 17 is mostly about respecting this.

2. Strength, in plain language

Rope strength gets talked about in one number and understood in none. Here are the only three ideas you need:

  • Breaking strength — the load at which brand-new rope, pulled slowly and straight in a lab, snaps. It's printed on good rope and straps. It is a ceiling, not a promise.
  • Working load — what you should actually hang on it: roughly one third of the breaking strength for straps, and less for rope. The gap is your safety margin for knots, age, dirt, and bad luck.
  • Knots weaken rope. Every knot bends fibers hard, and a knotted rope is typically only about half to two-thirds as strong as the plain rope. The fancier the tangle, the weaker the line.

And the quiet killer: shock loading. A load that drops and jerks — a falling crate caught by a rope, a load bouncing on a cart — can hit with several times its own weight for an instant. Snug and steady beats strong and jerky, every time. Take up slack gently, keep loads from bouncing, and never let anything free-fall onto a line.

The math that keeps you honest: a strap rated 900 lb breaking strength has a working load around 300 lb. Two of them over a load don't give you permission to be sloppy — they give you a backup when one slips. Rate for the load, then add a strap for the surprise.

3. Judging rope you find

New rope is easy to judge: read the tag. Found rope is a witness with no ID — its history is written on its body, if you know how to read it. Rope comes in two families:

  • Natural fiber (manila, cotton, jute, sisal — plant stuff). Grips knots beautifully, feels rough and hairy, and rots. A natural rope that lived wet is a liar: it can look fine and snap at half load.
  • Synthetic (nylon, polyester, polypropylene — plastic stuff). Nylon stretches (good for absorbing jerks, bad for precise tying-down). Polyester stretches less and shrugs off sun and weather — the best all-round found rope. Polypropylene is the cheap, floaty, orange-ish stuff: slippery, sun-rots fast, fine for bundling, never for anything heavy.

The bend test: fold a tight U in the rope and squeeze. Good rope resists and springs back. Dying rope crackles, stays kinked, or shows broken fibers at the fold. Trust the bend test over your eyes.

4. The parts of a knot

Sailors gave every part of a rope a name, not to be fancy, but because "the end — no, the other end" gets old fast. Four words unlock every instruction in this book:

  • Working end — the end in your hand, doing the tying. Sometimes called the running end.
  • Standing end — the rest of the rope, going off to the load or the coil. It stands still; the working end does the dancing.
  • Bight — a fold: any U-shape in the middle of a rope, without crossing.
  • Loop — a bight that crosses over itself, making a circle. Almost every knot starts here.

Two habits make every knot in this book work. Dress it: after tying, neaten the knot — pull each strand into its proper place so it looks like the picture, no twists or crossed bits. Set it: pull it snug, hard, before the load ever arrives. A sloppy knot isn't a weaker version of the knot; it's a different, worse knot that hasn't decided how it will fail yet.

5. Stoppers: the overhand and the figure-eight

A stopper knot is a lump tied in the end of a rope so the end can't slip through a hole, a pulley, or your other knots. Two of them cover everything:

  • Overhand knot — the first knot every toddler ties: make a loop, poke the end through, pull. Tiny, quick, and jams so hard under load that it's nearly permanent. Use it where you never plan to untie.
  • Figure-eight — the overhand's better brother: make a loop, wrap the end around the standing part, then through the loop. Bigger lump, stronger, and — the whole point — it can still be untied after a load. This is the stopper to learn.

When not to use them: a stopper is not a joining knot and not a hitch — it connects a rope to nothing but itself. And leave a hand's length of tail after any stopper: knots creep a little under load, and a stopper that creeps off the end is just a story.

6. The bowline: the king of loops

The bowline (say "bow-lin") makes a loop at the end of a rope that will not slip, will not shrink under load, and can still be untied afterwards. It has been the sailor's favorite for centuries for one job: a fixed loop you can trust — around a post, through a tarp's corner ring, on the end of a line you're hauling with.

The story every sailor learns it by: the loop is a rabbit hole. The working end is the rabbit. The standing part is the tree.

  1. Make a small loop in the rope, a hand's width from where you want the big loop to finish. The rabbit hole.
  2. The rabbit comes up through the hole — working end up through the small loop, leaving as much slack beyond it as the big loop needs to be.
  3. The rabbit runs around the tree — working end around behind the standing part.
  4. The rabbit goes back down the hole. Hold the big loop, pull the standing part, and the knot snugs up. Dress it: the finished knot should look neat, with a tail at least a hand long.

Use it for: a loop that must not close up — rescuing gear (not people), hanging a bucket, tying to a post you can't reach around twice. Never for: climbing or holding a person (it can loosen when unloaded and wiggled — sailors back it up with a stopper knot in the tail, and so should you for any load that matters), and never in slippery plastic rope without that backup stopper.

7. Hitches: tying a rope to a thing

A hitch ties a rope to an object — a post, a rail, a ring. Two hitches cover nearly every job in the aftermath:

  • Two half hitches — the workhorse. Pass the rope around the post, then tie the working end around the standing part twice, each time the same simple loop (a "half hitch"). Snug both down together. It's how tarps, clotheslines, and hammock ends have been hung forever. It holds, and it unties without a fight.
  • Clove hitch — two crossing wraps around a post that pinch each other tight. Fast to tie, easy to adjust by sliding, and the classic way to start a lashing (Entry 13). Its weakness is honesty-worthy: on a smooth post, with a load that twists or jerks, it can slowly work loose. Finish it with a half hitch when the load matters.

The rule for both: a hitch holds because the load pulls it tighter. If the load can push, twist, or go slack-and-jerk, add a backup — a stopper in the tail, or a second hitch. Slack is where hitches go to die.

8. The taut-line hitch: the loop that slides, then grips

Every tarp and tent line needs the same trick: a loop you can slide to tighten the line, that then grips and stays. That is the taut-line hitch — a hitch tied in the line itself, around its own standing part. Slide it away from the anchor and the guyline goes tight; let the load pull and the wraps bite and hold.

Use it for: tent and tarp guylines, clotheslines, any line you need to tension by hand and re-tension later — lines go slack as rope stretches and ground softens, and this is the knot that takes the slack back.

Never for: holding a person, or in slippery plastic rope without a third wrap inside the loop — it can creep. And it only grips when the wraps are dressed neat and the load pulls along the line; a sideways jerk can roll it loose.

9. The timber hitch: dragging logs and pipes

To drag a log, a pipe, or a bundle of poles, you want a hitch that tightens itself around the load and lets go the moment the load is down. The timber hitch is exactly that: around the log, around its own standing part, then twisted around its own strand — and the harder you pull, the harder it grips.

Use it for: dragging logs, pipes, and bundles lengthwise, and as the starting hitch for a diagonal lashing (Volume II). For a long load, add one plain half hitch around the log near the front end — it stops the load steering sideways as it drags.

Never for: lifting a load overhead — it is a dragging hitch, and it is designed to spill the moment the tension comes off. Keep the twists neat: three or four, side by side, or it can jam or slip instead of gripping.

10. The trucker's hitch: a pulley made of string

This is the single most useful tie in the book — the one that makes a rope tight, guitar-string tight, with no tools. Truckers used it to crush loads down before ratchet straps existed, and it works on the same trick as a pulley: trade distance for force. Pull with 20 pounds of effort and the rope pulls back with roughly three times that (minus friction's cut).

  1. Tie one end of the rope to an anchor — a post, a rail, the far side of the load — with two half hitches.
  2. Run the rope over (or around) the load to the near anchor. Partway along, tie a fixed loop in the rope — a figure-eight on a bight works, or a slip loop you can untie later.
  3. Pass the working end around the near anchor, then up through that loop.
  4. Pull down. The rope zig-zags, the load crushes down, and everything goes drum-tight.
  5. Pinch the tension with one hand and lock it off with two half hitches around the tight line. Done — and it unties like it was never there.

Protect the load: a drum-tight rope will cut into cardboard, foam, and soft wood. Slip a folded rag, a glove, or a chunk of cardboard under the rope wherever it crosses an edge. Tension is the point; damage is not.

11. Joining two ropes: the sheet bend

Sooner or later every rope is too short. Joining ropes is its own knot family, called bends, and one bend does almost all of it:

  • Sheet bend — make a bight in the end of the thicker rope. Pass the thinner rope's end up through the bight, around behind both legs of it, and tuck it under itself. Pull all four strands snug. It joins ropes of different thicknesses, which is exactly the found-rope situation, and it's how sailors join a line to a sheet (a sail's rope) — hence the name. For slippery plastic rope, wrap the thin end around twice before tucking (a double sheet bend).

THE SQUARE KNOT WARNING: the square (reef) knot — the flat, pretty knot from scout posters, left-over-right then right-over-left — is for parcels and bandages only. Under a real load it can roll over and spill itself open without warning. Never join load-bearing ropes with it, and never let it hold anything you'd miss. If you tied it by habit, untie it and tie a sheet bend.

And a word for the granny knot — the lopsided tangle you get when you repeat the same side twice tying your shoes. It's not a knot so much as an accident with good PR. If a knot looks drunk, it is: untie it and start over, dressed properly.

12. The reef knot (square knot): pretty, flat, and not for loads

This is the flat knot from every scout poster — right over left, then left over right — and it earns its own entry because you will tie it by habit if nobody shows you properly. Tied right, it sits flat and neat, both tails coming out on the same side as their own standing parts. Tied wrong — same side twice — it is a granny knot, lopsided and weaker, and it looks drunk. Untie it and start over.

Use it for: tying parcels, bundles, and bandages — jobs where the two ends are the same cord, the load is light, and a flat knot that will not dig in is the whole point.

Never for: joining ropes that hold anything that matters — under a real load it can roll over and spill itself open without warning, which is why Entry 11 carries the square knot warning. Join ropes with a sheet bend. This one is for packages, not loads.

13. Tying loads down, and lashing

Everything in Entries 5–12 comes together here, in the aftermath's most common job: making a pile of stuff stay on a cart, a roof, a trailer, or a sled.

  • Two straps or ropes, minimum, crossing. One tie lets a load rotate and walk. Two, pulling in different directions, argue it into staying.
  • Tighten in turns. Snug the first tie, then the second, then go back and re-snug the first — the second tie always steals a little tension from the first.
  • Check at the first stop, and after any bump. Loads settle. Rope that was tight at the door is loose a mile later. This is the step everyone skips and the step that matters most.
  • Mind the edges. Rope and straps die on sharp corners, and loads die under cutting straps. Pad every sharp edge with cardboard, cloth, or a split piece of hose.

Lashing is tying poles together — for a tripod, a frame, a stretcher, a shelter skeleton. The beginner's version, square lashing: start with a clove hitch on one pole, wrap the rope around both poles in a neat square pattern four or five times (keep every wrap snug and side-by-side, not crossed), take two tight turns between the poles over the wraps (that's "frapping" — it cinches everything drum-tight), and finish with a clove hitch. Neat wraps aren't vanity: crossed, sloppy wraps are weak wraps.

The wiggle test, again: when you think you're done, grab the load and try to move it like you're mad at it. Whatever moves in your driveway would have moved at speed, in the dark, in the rain. Fix it now, at walking pace, in daylight.

14. Straps: cam straps vs ratchet straps

Straps are webbing with hardware doing the knot's job. Two kinds fill every garage in the world, and they are not interchangeable:

Cam strapRatchet strap
How it tightensYou pull the loose end through a springy toothed buckle; your arm is the motorA handle cranks webbing onto a spool, click by click, with mechanical advantage
How tightSnug — as tight as you can pullCrushingly tight — tighter than the load may want
Best forKayaks, canoes, light and fragile loads, anything you'd crushHeavy, solid loads: furniture, machines, crates, vehicles
Watch outTeeth wear and cheap buckles slip; tie off the loose endEasy to over-tighten and bend or crack the load; more steps to use and to release

Both kinds share two rules. Flat, not twisted: a strap pulled flat is strong and kind; a strap with twists in it is weaker and saws at the load. One neat half-twist in a long span is an old trick to stop highway buzzing — but flat is the default. And read the tag: real straps are printed with a working load. No tag, no trust beyond light duty.

15. Ratchet straps: how to use one

The ratchet strap is the strongest tie in this book and the one beginners tangle most. It has three parts: the fixed end (a short strap with a hook, bolted to the ratchet body), the loose end (the long strap with the other hook), and the ratchet itself — a handle and a winding spool (the metal bar with a slot through it) between two toothed wheels.

  1. Hook both ends to anchor points, ratchet sitting where you can reach it, strap lying flat across the load.
  2. Thread it: open the handle up. Push the loose end's tail up through the slot in the spool, and pull nearly all the slack through by hand.
  3. Take up the slack first. The ratchet only needs to wind on the last bit — two or three wraps of strap around the spool is plenty. A spool stuffed fat with webbing jams and weakens.
  4. Crank the handle back and forth. Each swing clicks and winds the strap tighter. Stop at drum-tight: solid load, strap sings when plucked, nothing shifts when shoved.
  5. Lock it: close the handle flat against the body until it clicks. Tidy the loose tail — tie it off or tuck it so it can't flap itself undone or into a wheel.
  6. To release: pull the release lever inside the handle and open the handle all the way flat — 180 degrees — until it clicks open. Now the spool spins free and the strap pulls straight out. Never fight a loaded ratchet with pliers or a cheater bar; if it won't open flat, it won't release, and that's the mechanism telling you something.

Over-tight is a real failure. A ratchet doesn't know when to stop, and it can happily bend a bike frame, crack a plastic tote, or crush the corner of the thing you're saving. Tighten until the load can't move — then stop. The goal is a load that stays, not a strap that wins.

16. When the strap comes off the ratchet: putting it back

It will happen. You'll release a strap, pull a little too cheerfully, and the whole long strap will slide off the spool and land in a heap — leaving you holding a bare ratchet and a separate strap, feeling betrayed. Nothing is broken. The strap was only ever threaded through one slot. Here's the fix, in full:

  1. Open the ratchet all the way flat. Pull the release lever inside the handle and swing the handle open a full 180 degrees, until it clicks and stays. Now the spool can spin freely — this is the same position you use to release a strap, and it's the only position that lets you re-thread one.
  2. Find the slot. Turn the spool (the round bar between the toothed wheels) until you can see the slot — the long, thin opening that runs across it. Line the slot up so you can reach it, roughly facing you and slightly up.
  3. Feed the strap up through the slot, from underneath. Take the plain end of the loose strap — no hook end, no twists — and push it up through the slot from below, the same direction it went the first time. If it won't push, fold the end over once to stiffen it, or trim a frayed end square with scissors.
  4. Pull a hand's length through — about six inches of tail past the spool. That's all. The tail is what the spool will grab.
  5. Close the handle partway and start cranking. Swing the handle back toward closed and pump it. The spool turns, catches the tail, and winds the strap on over itself. Two or three wraps and it's captive again — it cannot come off while there's tension on it.
  6. Now use it normally (Entry 15): hook both ends to the load and crank to tension. The strap is captive again — it can't slide off while it's wound on. Just don't crank a mountain of strap onto the spool: two or three wraps under tension is right, and if the spool is stuffed fat, open it flat, pull the excess back out, and re-thread with less.

While you're in there: check the spool and teeth for rust and grit, and check the strap where it wraps the spool — that's where ratchet straps wear out first. A strap that's cut, frayed through a third of its width at any point, or sun-bleached stiff is retired, not repaired. Tape is not a repair.

17. Bungee cords, and little ties

Bungees earn their place exactly once: holding something light and bulky snug — a tarp's edge, a bundle of tarps or blankets, a lid that wants to flap. For that, they're wonderful. Everything else about them demands respect:

  • A stretched bungee is a loaded spring with a metal hook on the end. When (not if) one lets go, the hook comes back at face speed. The classic bungee injury is to the eye. Hook and unhook with your face turned away and your free hand shielding — every time, like it's a rule, because it is one now.
  • Never stretch one past about twice its resting length — beyond that it's near snapping and barely holding anyway.
  • Never the only thing holding a load on a moving cart or vehicle. Bungees stretch over bumps, which means the load moves, which means the bungee stretches more. Use rope or straps for the load; bungees tidy the edges.
  • Check the jacket. Frayed cloth showing pale, cracked rubber inside means the spring is dying. Retire it before it introduces itself to your face.

Little ties — the small stuff that finishes a thousand jobs:

  • Zip ties — instant, strong for their size, and one-way: once clicked, the only edit is cutting. Carry two sizes. Two zip ties looped through each other make a longer one. They go brittle in cold and sun, so they're a repair, not a structure.
  • Wire — picture wire, rebar tie wire, a stripped scrap of copper: twist it with pliers and it holds like a tiny clamp. It fixes fences, hangs pots, and stitches broken plastic. Wire ends are sharp — fold them flat when you're done.
  • Improvised ties — torn cloth strips (stronger than you'd think, and they grip), strips cut from an inner tube (rubber bands with ambitions), a belt, a shoelace, duct tape wrapped around a bundle. The aftermath rule: a tie that holds and can be checked beats the perfect tie you don't have.

18. What to scavenge for rope and ties

Tie-stuff is everywhere in the ruins, and most of it is free for the picking because nobody else thinks it's treasure:

  • Clothesline and paracord — laundry rooms, camping kits, flag poles, broken tents (tent guy-lines are good cord with free tensioners attached).
  • Seatbelts — dead cars are full of the strongest webbing you'll ever find, with buckles. Cut long; a seatbelt is a tow-worthy strap.
  • Ratchet and cam straps — truck beds, garages, moving vans. Check them like Entry 16 says: webbing first, hardware second.
  • Bungees — garages and car trunks, by the tangled dozen. Untangle, inspect the jackets, retire the frayed ones on the spot.
  • Extension cords and wire — a dead extension cord is fifty feet of light-duty tie line and a source of copper wire. (It's a tie now, not a cable — never re-energize a cord you've cut. Book 1's rules still apply.)
  • Inner tubes — cut into strips, they're heavy-duty rubber ties that grip and stretch. Bike shops and tire piles.
  • Zip ties, tape, baling wire — junk drawers and workshops. Small, light, and worth their weight in finished repairs.
  • Climbing and marine rope in a former life — grab it for heavy utility work, coil it, and remember Entry 3: unknown history means never for a person, no matter how good it looks.

19. Caring for rope: coil it, keep it alive

Rope dies of neglect, not work. Four habits double its life:

  • Coil it right. The simple over-under coil: loop the rope, and alternate each loop's twist — one loop rolled one way, the next the other — so the coil hangs flat without kinks. Or just butterfly-coil a long line (back and forth across your palm and elbow in figure-eights) and wrap the middle. A rope stored in a tangled bird's nest is a rope that's already half-retired.
  • Keep it dry, dark, and off the ground. Sun eats synthetics, damp rots naturals, and concrete floors wick moisture and grit into everything. A hook, a bag, a bucket with a lid: luxury accommodations, by rope standards.
  • Clean the grit out. Dirt inside a rope is sandpaper working from the inside. Shake it, whip it against a post, wash it in plain water if it's filthy, and dry it fully before storing.
  • Finish the ends. Cut synthetic rope melts and frays — melt the end with a lighter until it beads, or wrap it tight with tape or string (that's "whipping"). A frayed end is a rope slowly untying itself.
  • Retire it honestly. Stiff, glazed, frayed-through, or mystery-stained with chemicals? Cut it into short utility lengths for bundling, mark it, and never let it hold anything that matters again.

Field recipes: put it all together

Four jobs that use this whole book at once. Each one names the knot or strap for every tie — if a step just says "tie it," it isn't finished.

Field Recipe · Tarp Shelter Ridgeline

Makes: a taut line between two trees with a tarp hung over it — the aftermath's first roof.

You need: one long rope, one tarp, two short cords for the corners.

  1. Tie one end of the long rope to the first tree with two half hitches (Entry 7), chest high.
  2. Run the rope to the second tree and finish with a trucker's hitch (Entry 10). Crank it until the line hums.
  3. Drape the tarp over the line, centered. Tie each corner down to a stake or a heavy object with a figure-eight stopper in the cord end and a half hitch at the stake.
  4. Low side toward the wind. A sagging corner is a rain bucket — re-tension after the first hour.

Field Recipe · Clothesline That Won't Sag

Makes: a line that stays tight under a full load of wet clothes — wet denim is heavier than you think.

You need: one rope, two solid anchors (posts, rails, tree trunks).

  1. Anchor the first end with a bowline (Entry 6) around the post — it will not creep.
  2. Anchor the second end with a trucker's hitch (Entry 10) and pull it drum-tight before you hang anything.
  3. Hang the heaviest items at the ends, lightest in the middle. The middle is where every line sags first.
  4. After ten minutes under load, take up one more pull on the trucker's hitch. Rope stretches; plan for it.

Field Recipe · Lash a Load to a Roof or Rack

Makes: a load that is still on the roof when you arrive — checked the hard way, at home.

You need: two ratchet straps (or two ropes), padding for every sharp edge.

  1. Pad the load's sharp corners first (Entry 13). Straps die on edges before they die of age.
  2. Strap one across the load, hook to hook, and crank it snug — not savage (Entry 15). If a strap has come off its ratchet, re-thread it first (Entry 16).
  3. Strap two crosses the first at a different angle. Two ties arguing in different directions is what holds a load (Entry 13).
  4. Tie off both loose tails. Then the wiggle test: shove the load like you're mad at it. Whatever moves now would have moved at speed.

Field Recipe · Hoist a Bucket to a Roof or Balcony

Makes: heavy things going up a wall without anyone carrying them up a ladder.

You need: one rope long enough to reach the ground twice over, one bucket with a handle, one person up top, one on the ground.

  1. Up top: tie the rope's end to a solid anchor with two half hitches, and drop the rest of the rope to the ground. Nobody holds a load with just their hands.
  2. On the ground: tie the bucket handle with a bowline (Entry 6) — the loop will not close up and crush the handle, and it unties after.
  3. Add a figure-eight stopper in the tail as a backup, the way sailors back up a bowline.
  4. Person up top hauls hand over hand; the ground person guides and keeps clear — never stand under a load, ever. Lower it the same way, slowly.

Field Recipe · Bundle and Carry Firewood with a Rope Sling

Makes: an armload of sticks turned into one bundle with a handle — one trip instead of four, and both hands stay in the deal.

ComponentCount
Rope, about an arm-span and a half long×1
Firewood, sticks of similar length1 bundle
Work gloves — bark and splinters are real1 pair
  1. Lay the rope straight on the ground and pile the sticks across its middle, longest sticks in the center, ends roughly even.
  2. Lift both rope ends up over the pile and tie a timber hitch (Entry 9) around the whole bundle. It bites tighter the harder you lift — that is the point of it.
  3. Take the two tails and tie them together with a reef knot (Entry 12), leaving a loop big enough for a hand or a shoulder. That loop is your handle.
  4. Test-lift the bundle a few inches and shake it once. Any stick that slides out goes back in across the middle, never along the edge.
  5. Carry it with the handle over your shoulder, bundle resting at your hip. Set it down by the tails, untie the reef knot, and the sling is ready for the next load.

Field Recipe · Hang a Food Bag Out of Reach

Makes: supper hanging high, out past the end of a branch — too high for a raccoon's patience and too far out for anything that climbs the trunk.

ComponentCount
Rope — at least twice the height of your branch×1
Food bag or dry sack, with a loop or handle×1
Throw weight — a rock in an old sock works×1
Tree with a high, sturdy, living branch×1
  1. Pick the branch first: living, thick as your arm at the trunk, and high enough that the bag will hang about 4 metres off the ground and a good metre and a half out from the trunk.
  2. Tie the throw weight to one end of the rope with two half hitches (Entry 7). Toss it over the branch, out near where the bag should hang, and let the weight pull the rope down.
  3. Untie the weight. Tie the bag on with a bowline (Entry 6) through its loop or handle, and back it up with a figure-eight stopper (Entry 5) in the tail.
  4. Haul the bag up to height. Tie the standing end off to the trunk with a trucker's hitch (Entry 10) pulled snug, then lock it with two half hitches so nothing creeps in the night.
  5. In the morning, reverse it: untie, lower hand over hand, and never stand directly under the bag while it moves.

20. Stop signs

  • Any rope, strap, or knot holding a person. Not climbing, not lowering, not a swing, not a harness. Found gear has no history you can trust and this book's knots are for loads of things. People stay on the ground or on rated, inspected gear with someone trained holding the other end.
  • Frayed, cut, sun-bleached, or brittle line under load. Damage you can see means damage you can't. Retire it or cut back to clean rope — never "one more trip" it.
  • Shock loading. Anything that can fall, bounce, or jerk against a line multiplies its own weight. Take up slack gently, keep loads from bouncing, and never stand where a snapping line will swing.
  • Standing in the bite. Never stand inside a loop of rope under tension, in line with a strap that's being cranked, or downhill of a load that could roll. When a tie fails, everything moves at once, along the line. Be somewhere else.
  • A square knot joining ropes under load. It spills open. Sheet bend, every time (Entry 11).
  • A bungee aimed at a face. Hook and unhook turned away, hand shielding, never stretched past double. Eye injuries from bungee hooks are common, fast, and permanent.
  • A strap over a sharp edge, or a load tied with one tie. Edges cut, single ties let loads walk. Pad the edge, cross the ties, check at the first stop.
  • Over-cranking a ratchet. The mechanism will happily crush the thing you're trying to save. Drum-tight, then stop.

Every stop sign in this book is the same sign, really: a tie under tension is stored energy with a plan you didn't make. Respect the tension, check the tie, and stay out of its line of fire.

21. The whole book on one card

  • Rope is round and thick, cord is thin, webbing is flat, and a bungee is a spring — never confuse the four.
  • Working load is about a third of breaking strength, and a knot costs you half the rope's strength before you start.
  • Judge found rope with the bend test: crackly, kinked, frayed, or musty means leave it.
  • Working end, standing end, bight, loop — dress every knot neat and set it hard.
  • Figure-eight is the stopper. Bowline is the fixed loop: up through the hole, around the tree, back down.
  • Overhand is the quick stopper that jams permanent; the figure-eight is the one that unties.
  • Two half hitches hang things; a clove hitch starts lashings; a trucker's hitch makes rope drum-tight with no tools.
  • Taut-line hitch slides to tighten a guyline, then grips. Timber hitch drags a log and lets go when you set it down.
  • Join ropes with a sheet bend. Never a square knot under load.
  • Reef (square) knot: parcels and bandages only — flat, pretty, and never for a load.
  • Two ties, crossed, padded at the edges, checked at the first stop.
  • Ratchet strap: thread up through the spool slot, pull slack by hand, crank, close flat to lock, open flat to release.
  • Strap came off the ratchet? Open it flat, find the slot, feed the end up through from underneath, pull a hand's length through, crank it back on.
  • Bungees tidy edges; they never hold the load. Face away from the hooks.
  • Coil it, keep it dry and dark, finish the ends, retire it honestly.
