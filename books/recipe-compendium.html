Makes: one 12V light running straight off a battery — Job One, on a card.

ComponentCount
12V battery×1

Switch — any kind that breaks the plus wire×1
12V lamp or LED light×1
Copper wire, enough to make the whole circle2 runs
  1. Build it dead — battery disconnected. Run the plus wire: battery plus → fuse → switch → lamp.
  2. Run the lamp’s other wire straight back to battery minus. The circle must be complete.
  3. The fuse sits on the plus wire, close to the battery.
  4. Walk the circle with your finger and check every joint.

Field Recipe · The Suitcase Rig

Makes: a portable 12V power station in a tote — lights, phones, and a radio, anywhere.

ComponentCount
Solar panel, 100W×1
Charge controller, 20A MPPT (or PWM)×1
Battery, 12V 35Ah sealed (AGM)×1

  1. Pad the tote so the battery cannot slide — cardboard or foam. A sliding battery is a short waiting to happen.
  2. Mount the controller inside the lid. Wire controller → battery first — fuse on the plus wire, close to the battery — and set the battery type.

Field Recipe · Battery Bank in a Box

Makes: a two-battery 12V bank, 1,200 usable Wh, boxed and fused — the heart of a stay-put rig.

ComponentCount
Deep-cycle batteries, 12V 100Ah — twins: same type, age, and charge×2
Vented box or crate, bigger than the pair×1
Thick battery cable for the jumpers — the fattest you have2 runs
Main fuse + holder, sized to your biggest load's wire×1
Terminal lugs + bolts1 set
  1. Charge both batteries separately first, and check them: rested volts within 0.2V of each other, or they are not twins yet. Never chain a full battery to a flat one.
  2. Set them side by side in the box, terminals up, a hand's width of air around them. Flooded batteries vent gas — the lid rests loose or the box is vented, never sealed.
  3. Chain parallel: plus to plus, minus to minus, with the fat jumpers (Entry 8).
  4. Take the bank's main leads off opposite ends — plus from battery A, minus from battery B — so both share every charge and discharge evenly.
  5. Main fuse on the plus lead, close to the terminal. Label the box: 200Ah · 12V · 1,200 usable Wh and the date. Every future calculation starts from that label.

Field Recipe · Tilt Stand from Scrap

Makes: an adjustable wooden stand that aims a panel at your latitude angle — free watts, forever, for the price of two boards.

ComponentCount
Solar panel, any size×1
Scrap planks or 2×4 offcuts, two long + one prop leg×3
Hinges, or bolts to pivot on×2
Screws1 handful
Rope + a ground stake, for wind insurance×1
  1. Screw the two long planks to the panel's back frame rails (the frame has holes for this) so they stick out below the panel like skis.
  2. Hinge the prop leg to the top rail. The leg sets the tilt: leg length and where it lands decide the angle — aim for your latitude (Entry 15). Mark two notches: SUMMER and WINTER (winter = latitude + 15°).
  3. Face it at the equator — south if you're north — where the day-long shadow watch says the sky stays clear.
  4. Stake the rope over the frame. A panel is a sail with a glass face; the first storm will collect any stand that isn't tied down.
  5. Re-aim with the seasons. Five minutes, twice a year, worth more than a second panel in winter.

Field Recipe · All-Night Porch Light

Makes: a light that charges all day and lights your door all night, by itself, forever — a garden light, grown up.

ComponentCount
Solar panel, 20–50W×1
Charge controller, 10A, with a load terminal and dusk setting×1
Battery, 12V 12–20Ah sealed×1
12V LED light or strip, 5–10W×1
Inline fuse holder + 5A fuse×1
Wire, enough for panel-to-door1 run
  1. The trick of this build: most small controllers have a load terminal that can switch on at dusk — the controller watches the panel go dark and turns the light on for you. Set it to dusk-to-dawn (or a fixed 8 hours to be kind to the battery in winter).
  2. Wire in the sacred order: controller → battery (fuse on plus), light → load terminal, panel last.
  3. Do the math once: 10W × 12 hours = 120Wh a night, and a 20Ah battery gives ≈120 usable Wh — exactly one night. The panel refills it by day (50W × 4 × 0.75 = 150Wh). Balanced.
  4. Mount the panel where the light's glow can't reach it — a controller that sees the light may think it's dawn and switch off. Comedy, once. Then move the panel.

Field Recipe · Char Cloth

Makes: black cloth squares that catch a single spark and hold a smolder for minutes — the bridge between a ferro rod and a flame.

ComponentCount
100% cotton cloth (old t-shirt, denim), cut in squares×6
Small metal tin with a lid, one nail-hole poked in the top×1
  1. Cloth squares go in the tin, lid on. The tin goes in a fire or on coals.
  2. Smoke jets out of the hole while the cloth cooks without burning — there's no air in the tin, so it chars instead of catching.
  3. When the smoke stops (about 10 minutes), pull the tin out and let it cool completely before opening. Open it hot and the cloth flashes to ash in the fresh air.
  4. Done right, the squares are black, limp, and still cloth-shaped. Store them dry in the tin.

Field Recipe · Rocket Stove

Makes: a stove that boils a pot on a handful of sticks — the most cooking per twig of anything in this book.

ComponentCount
Large can (#10 can or a clean paint can) — the body×1
Smaller cans — one for the vertical chimney, one for the sideways feed tube×2
Ash, sand, or dirt — insulation to pack between the tubes and the body1 fill
A few sticks or a grate — a pot stand for the top×1
  1. Cut a hole low in the big can's side for the feed tube, and a hole in its top for the chimney.
  2. Join the two small cans into an L: the feed tube points sideways, the chimney rises from its inner end, up through the big can's top hole.
  3. Pack ash or sand into every gap between the L and the big can. Insulation is what makes it a rocket instead of a can.
  4. Light a small fire at the L's elbow. Feed thumb-thick sticks in through the side tube, a little at a time. The chimney pulls air through and the flame roars straight up at the pot.

Field Recipe · The Barrel Raft

Makes: a working cargo platform for four drums' worth of lift — about 800 honest pounds of people and freight, in one afternoon.

ComponentCount
Sealed plastic drums, bungs tight, no cracks — your flotation and your life4
Deck boards or two pallets — the floor everything stands on6–8
Long rails, stout — the sandwich that traps the drums2
Rope for lashings — long enough to go around everything, twice30 m
A long pole or a board on a handle — the sweep that steers it1
  1. Lay the two rails parallel on the ground, a deck's width apart. Stand the four drums between them, two by two.
  2. Lay the deck boards across the top, overhanging the rails slightly. The drums are now trapped in a sandwich: rails below the deck, drums between, nothing able to escape sideways.
  3. Lash every crossing — rail to deck, deck to rail — in tight figure patterns, working around all four drums. Screws or bolts where you have them; lashings where you do not.

  5. Mount the sweep on a post at the stern, load the middle low and centered, and re-check the lashings after the first hour in the water.

Field Recipe · Ram Pump from Pipe Fittings

Makes: the pump that lifts water uphill day and night with no fuel — a stream's fall delivers a tenth of its water to a tank far above.

ComponentCount
Stout rigid pipe for the drive line, running downhill to the pump10 m+
Check valves — the two moving parts; one slams, one delivers. Spares are wisdom2
A sealed bottle or small tank — the air chamber that smooths the pulses into a flow1
Delivery hose or pipe — the long climb to the tank, narrower than the drive pipeas needed
A screen for the intake — keeps the stream's debris out of your valves1
  1. Site it: you need flow and a few feet of fall. The pump sits below the intake, at the bottom of the drive pipe's run.
  2. Assemble the classic cross: drive pipe in, waste valve (the slammer) on one branch, delivery valve and air chamber on the other, line climbing away.
  3. Open the water. Flow accelerates, the waste valve slams, and the hammer-shock shoves a pulse into the air chamber — a few dozen times a minute. Tune the waste valve until it ticks steadily like a clock.
  4. Run the delivery line up to the tank. Expect a tenth of the drive water to arrive, lifted far higher than the fall powering it.
  5. Check it weekly: clear the intake screen, feel for the steady tick. A silent ram is a clogged screen or a tired valve, and both are ten-minute fixes.

Field Recipe · Gravity Drip Line for a Garden Row

Makes: all-day watering for a garden row from one raised barrel — the irrigation of Entry 9, shrunk to fit a backyard and a bucket brigade.

ComponentCount
A barrel or drum — the reservoir; bigger is lazier1
Blocks, a stand, or a bank — height is pressure; a metre up is enough3–4
Garden hose — the drip line itself, one per row1 per row
Cloth for a filter at the outlet — keeps the drip holes dripping instead of clogging1 piece
An awl, nail, or small drill — makes the drip holes; smaller than you think1
  1. Set the barrel high at the row's end — on blocks, a stand, or the bank above the garden. Height is the whole pump.
  2. Fit the outlet low on the barrel with the cloth filter inside it. Fill the barrel and let the sediment settle away from the outlet.

Field Recipe · Tarp Coracle

Makes: a one-person bowl boat you can carry under one arm — a river crossing, a fishing perch, and a thousand years of precedent.

ComponentCount
Springy saplings or willow wands — the ribs of the bowl; green wood bends, dry wood snaps8–10
A tarp, roughly two metres square — the skin; no holes, grommets a bonus1
Cord for lashing — the rim is lashed, not nailed; it must flex10 m
A hoop or stout rim wand — the gunwale that gives the bowl its shape and its carry handle1
A paddle — one blade, used over the bow in a figure-eight, coracle fashion1
  1. Bend the rim hoop into a circle on the ground and lash the join. This circle is the boat's mouth and its destiny — a metre to a metre and a half across.
  2. Bend the saplings into U shapes, ends lashed around the hoop, bellies crossing under the center — a basket's skeleton, ribs in both directions.

  5. Launch in the shallows, kneel in the center, and learn the spin in water you can stand up in — before the river teaches you at tuition rates.

Field Recipe · Oral Rehydration Salts (ORS)

Makes: one litre of the fluid that pulls people back from dehydration — the highest-value recipe in this book.

ComponentCount
Clean water — boiled, then cooled (Entry 9)1 litre
Sugar — level teaspoons, not heaped6 tsp
Salt — level half-teaspoon. Measure like it matters; it does½ tsp
A spoon for measuring and stirring×1
  1. Dissolve the sugar and salt completely in the water. Cloudy is fine; gritty is not — keep stirring.
  2. Taste it. It should taste like tears — mildly salty, not like the sea. Saltier than tears means too much salt: pour it out and remake it. Too much salt in a dehydrated body is genuinely dangerous; the measuring is the medicine.

  4. Keep it coming for as long as fluid is being lost, and after, until the pee runs pale (Entry 9).
  5. Make it fresh daily. Yesterday's batch, kept covered and cool, is acceptable; three days old is a germ farm wearing a halo.

Stop signs

  • Clear is not clean. The water recipes strain and catch — the kill step from Safe Water Management (boil, bleach, or sun) still has to happen. Never let a recipe on this shelf talk you out of it.
  • Anything here that burns, burns by the Fire book's rules. Outdoors or up a sound chimney, never in a sealed room, never left alone, never slept beside. Carbon monoxide does not care that you were only following a recipe.
  • Electrical recipes are built dead. Battery disconnected while you work, fuse on the plus wire close to the battery, battery connected last — exactly as Basic Wiring and Electricity teaches.
  • Rope recipes hold things, not people. No recipe on this shelf turns found rope into climbing, lowering, or harness gear for a human. Gear only, every time.
  • A swapped ingredient is a different recipe. Self-lighting charcoal for plain charcoal, synthetic cloth for cotton, a mystery rope for a sound one — change the ingredient and the steps may no longer be true. Go back to the source book and check.
  • If a page here and its home book ever disagree, the home book wins. This shelf copies; the originals rule.
