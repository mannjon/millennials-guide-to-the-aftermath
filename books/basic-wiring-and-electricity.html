DON'T PANIC

Basic Wiring and Electricity, Explained

Start here: the grid is gone

The wall outlet is a decoration now. The power company is not coming. But power itself is not gone — it is sitting in every car battery, every solar panel, every coil of copper wire in every abandoned garage. This book teaches you to build a small, safe power rig out of that stuff, assuming you have never wired anything in your life. Because maybe you haven't. That's fine. Nobody is born knowing this. They just usually learn it before the world ends.

This book is about battery power only — the 12-volt kind that runs cars, camp lights, and radios. House wiring is a different book and a bigger danger. Everything here is the gentle end of electricity. Gentle, not harmless: a battery can still start a fire if you treat it stupidly. So we go slow, in order, one wire at a time.

1. Electricity, in one picture

Pretend electricity is water in a pipe. That's it. That's the whole trick.

  • Volts = water pressure. How hard it pushes. A car battery pushes at 12 volts.
  • Amps = how much water is flowing right now.
  • Watts = pressure × flow. The total work getting done. This is the number printed on every light and charger.
  • Ohms = how skinny the pipe is. Skinny wire, long wire, bad connections — all squeeze the flow.

In real life: a battery is a water tower. It stores push. Your job in this book is to run a pipe from the tower to something useful — a light, a radio, a phone charger — and back again, without springing a leak.

2. The one rule that keeps you safe

BUILD IT DEAD. CONNECT THE BATTERY LAST. A battery has no off switch. It is awake right now, in the dark, waiting. So you assemble the whole circuit first — every wire, every connection — and the battery joins the party at the very end, one terminal at a time. Taking it apart? Battery comes off first.

Two more habits that do most of the safety work in this book:

  • One terminal at a time. Never let a tool, a loose wire end, or your own metal watch strap touch both battery terminals at once. That is a short circuit: all the flow, all at once, through your wrench. It can weld, spark, and burn. Take rings and bracelets off. Tape any wire end that isn't connected yet.
  • Every rig gets a fuse. Entry 4. No fuse, no power. The fuse is the one part designed to die so the rest of your rig doesn't.

In real life: 12 volts won't shock you through dry skin — you can touch both terminals of a car battery and feel nothing. What hurts people at 12 volts is heat: short circuits, glowing wires, fires in the dark. Respect the heat and this voltage is a friendly one.

3. A circuit is a circle

Here is the secret nobody leads with: power has to go home. Electricity leaves the battery's plus terminal (+), does its work, and must return to the minus terminal (−) to complete the loop. No loop, no flow, no light. Every circuit you will ever build is this one circle wearing different clothes.

  • Plus (+) — usually marked red. The door power leaves by.
  • Minus (−) — usually marked black. The door power comes home by.
  • The loop — if anything in the circle is broken, disconnected, or switched off, nothing flows. When a rig "just doesn't work," you are hunting for the break in the circle. It is always the break in the circle.

In real life: wire colors are a promise the last person made. On a battery, trust the + and − stamped in the plastic, not the color of whatever wire someone left attached. On scrap wire, you pick the colors — pick a rule (red tape on the plus wire) and never break it.

4. The fuse: the guard on your wire

A fuse is a deliberate weak link — a tiny strip of metal inside a little housing that melts on purpose if too much power flows. It dies in a millisecond so your wire doesn't die over ten seconds, glowing, inside your wall tent. Think of it as the guard from the water picture, except this guard's whole job is to throw himself on the grenade.

  • Where: on the plus wire, as close to the battery as you can put it. Everything you run goes through it.
  • What size: bigger than your load needs, smaller than your wire can carry. Running one 10-watt light (about 1 amp at 12 volts)? A 5-amp fuse is a calm, sensible guard. The fuse's number is printed on it.
  • If a fuse blows: something is wrong — a short, a pinched wire, too big a load. Find it. A bigger fuse is not a fix; it's removing the guard because he kept doing his job.

In real life: amps = watts ÷ volts. A 60-watt load on 12 volts pulls 5 amps. Do this division for everything you plug in, write the number on a piece of tape on the wire, and future-you will never have to guess.

5. What you need, and what to scavenge

  • A battery. Car, truck, lawn tractor, golf cart, boat — if it's a big 12-volt brick, it's a water tower. Bigger and heavier generally means more stored power.
  • Copper wire. The veins of everything below. How to judge scrap below.
  • A fuse holder and spare fuses. Sold in every auto parts store on Earth, and sitting in every dead car's fuse box right now. Take spares. Fuses are consumables; blowing them while learning is normal.
  • A switch. Any switch. It goes on the plus wire and becomes the gate.
  • Something useful to power. Start with a 12-volt LED light or strip. LEDs sip power and forgive beginners.
  • Electrical tape. For insulating joints and marking your plus wire with red tape.
  • A knife or wire strippers. For baring the copper ends.
  • A tester. A multimeter if you find one. If not, a spare 12-volt bulb on two wires is a test light: it glows, there's power; it doesn't, there isn't.
  • For charging later: a solar panel and a charge controller (Entry 7).

Judging scrap copper wire:

To use a wire: cut off damaged ends, strip about a finger-width of coat off each end, and twist the bare copper strands tight into one neat rope. A good joint is copper squeezed hard against copper — twist them together like you're wringing out a tiny towel, then tape the joint so no bare metal shows.

6. Job one: make one light work

Every power rig on Earth is this job plus more stuff. One battery, one fuse, one switch, one light, one circle.

The wiring sequence. In this order, every time:

  1. Build the whole circle with the battery left out. Battery → fuse → switch → lamp → a wire long enough to reach back to the battery's minus. Switch in the OFF position. Mark the plus wire with red tape at both ends.
  2. Check your joints. Every connection twisted tight, every bare spot taped, no stray copper hairs reaching toward anything they shouldn't.
  3. Connect the minus wire to the battery's minus terminal. Tight. A loose terminal is a heater you didn't ask for.
  4. Connect the plus wire to the plus terminal — last. This is the moment the rig wakes up. Keep the loose end taped until the second it goes on.
  5. Flip the switch. Light. You have built civilization's favorite trick from junk.

No light? Don't panic and don't rewire randomly. Walk the circle: battery charged? (Test light across the terminals.) Fuse intact? (The strip inside should be one unbroken piece.) Switch on? Every joint tight? The problem is a break in the circle — find the break, not a new design.

In real life: taking it apart runs the film backwards — battery off first (plus terminal first, and tape that end immediately), then everything else. The plus wire is the troublemaker; it only ever touches the plus terminal, the fuse, and nothing else in the world.

7. Job two: add the sun

A battery alone is a water tower that only empties. A solar panel is rain. The charge controller is the small box that stands between them — it feeds the battery when it's hungry and stops when it's full, because a battery force-fed past full gets cooked. Panel, controller, battery. That order of boxes, always.

The connection sequence:

  1. Controller to battery — FIRST. The controller wakes up, reads the battery, and learns "ah, a 12-volt system." Skip this order and a confused controller can feed your battery like it's a 24-volt one.
  2. Your stuff to the battery — second, through its fuse, exactly like Job One.
  3. Panel to controller — LAST. A panel makes power the moment light touches it; there is no off switch on the sun. Throw a blanket or a towel over the panel while you wire it, and it sleeps until you're done.
  • Never leave a panel wired straight to a battery with no controller between them. A day of that can overcharge and kill the battery you're depending on.
  • Panel facing the sun, tilted roughly toward it, nothing shading even one corner — shade on part of a panel drags the whole panel down.
  • Everything still goes in a circle: panel has a plus and a minus, and they land on the controller's panel plus and minus. Match the stamps, not your mood.

In real life: the panel's label tells you its watts. A 100-watt panel in good sun gives you roughly 400–500 watt-hours on a clear day — enough for lights, phones, and a radio, with some left over. Less sun, less rain in the tower. Entry 9 teaches you to budget it.

8. More power: series and parallel

One battery not enough? You can chain batteries (and panels — the same rules) in exactly two ways. They are not interchangeable, and mixing them up is the fastest way in this book to destroy equipment.

  • Series (nose-to-tail): voltages add. 12 + 12 = 24 volts. Only do this if your equipment is built for the bigger push — 24 volts into a 12-volt light is a brief, bright funeral.
  • Parallel (side by side): voltage stays, stored power adds. Two batteries in parallel run the same lights twice as long. This is the one most rigs want.
  • Only chain twins: same voltage, same size, same type, same age, both charged to the same level first. A weak battery in a chain drags the strong ones down to its level.
  • Tape a label on every battery — its voltage and the date you chained it. In the dark, in a hurry, labels are memory that works.

9. Budget your bucket

A battery is a bucket of stored work, and now you can measure the bucket. Battery labels brag in amp-hours (Ah). Multiply by volts and you get watt-hours (Wh) — the actual size of the bucket. A 12-volt, 100-amp-hour battery holds 12 × 100 = 1,200 watt-hours.

One catch: an old-style lead battery (car, golf cart) is only willing to give up about half its bucket before running it lower starts killing it. Lithium batteries will give you nearly the whole bucket. So that 1,200 Wh lead battery is really a 600 Wh daily budget if you want it alive next month.

The spending math is one multiplication: watts × hours = watt-hours.

That is the whole game of off-grid power: the panel is your income (Entry 7: a 100 W panel earns roughly 400–500 Wh on a clear day), the bucket is your savings, and every load is a purchase. Spend less than you earn and the lights never go out.

10. Wire thickness and distance: the two thieves

Back to the water picture: a long, skinny pipe wastes pressure before the water arrives. Wire does the same. Two thieves steal your power on every run, and both are beaten the same way:

  • Thief one: thin wire. Big flow through a skinny wire turns into heat inside the wire. The load gets weak power and you get a warm cable.
  • Thief two: distance. Every extra foot of wire is more squeeze. A light at the end of 50 feet of skinny wire glows like it's tired, because it is.
  • The defense: short and thick. Keep the battery close to the things it powers. For runs that must be long, go thicker than you think you need.
  • The ten-minute test: run the rig for ten minutes, then feel every wire and every joint with the back of your hand. Cool is correct. Warm means too thin, or a loose joint pretending to be a connection. Hot means stop now and fix it.

In real life: skip the inverter for as long as you can. An inverter turns battery power into house-style power, and it's a glutton: a 1,000-watt inverter pulls around 90 amps from a 12-volt battery — jumper-cable territory, thick and short or not at all. Lights, radios, fans, and chargers all exist in 12-volt versions. Live in the battery's world; don't make the battery pretend to be the grid.

11. Stop signs

  • A battery that is swollen, cracked, leaking, or hissing. Walk away from it. It is done being a battery and is becoming an incident.
  • A rotten-egg smell near a charging battery. That's gas from overcharging. Stop charging, air the space out, no sparks nearby.
  • A big flash or spark when you connect a terminal. Small loads can tick; a flash means a short. Battery off, walk the circle, find it.
  • Any wire too hot to hold, or a fuse that blows a second time. The rig is telling you something. Listen the first time.
  • Two batteries of different voltages someone wants to chain together "just to try it." No. Entry 8: twins only.
  • Anyone who wants to wire your rig into a house's breaker panel to "power the house." That is the other kind of electricity, the killing kind, and it is not in this book. Your rig powers its own loads through its own wires, full stop.
  • White crusty stuff on battery terminals is battery acid residue. Don't touch your eyes, wash your hands after, clean terminals with an old brush, not your good one.
  • The moment you think "I'm not sure." That thought is the skill working. Listen to it.

Knowing where your skill ends is most of having the skill.

12. The whole book on one card

  • Every circuit is a circle: out of the plus, through the work, home to the minus.
  • Build it dead. Battery on last, off first. One terminal at a time.
  • Fuse on the plus wire, close to the battery. No fuse, no power.
  • Switch on the plus wire. It's just a gate.
  • Solar: controller to battery first, panel last. Off in reverse.
  • Series adds push. Parallel adds runtime. Twins only.
  • Amps = watts ÷ volts. Watt-hours = watts × hours.
  • Short and thick beats long and thin. Warm wire = warning.
  • Not sure = stop.
