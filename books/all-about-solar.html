DON'T PANIC

All About Solar, Explained

The Millennial's Guide to the Aftermath

Start here: the one utility still working

The grid is gone, but the sun did not get the memo. It shows up every morning, on time, free, and it will keep showing up for roughly the next five billion years — the most reliable utility you will ever have. Every rooftop panel, every garden light, every RV roof in the ruins is a tiny power plant waiting for someone who knows how to plug it in.

This book is that knowledge. It assumes you have read Basic Wiring and Electricity, Explained — or that you at least know a circuit is a circle and a fuse is not optional. Here we go deeper on the sun side: what a panel really makes, what your stuff really eats, how to chain panels and batteries together without breaking the limits printed on the labels, what MPPT actually does, and the only two questions that matter in the end — how long will it take to charge, and how long will it run?

Everything here is arithmetic you can do on paper. No app, no internet, no guessing. If you can multiply and divide, you can size a solar rig that works the first time.

The whole book in one sentence: Watts in, watts out, watt-hours in the bucket — count all three honestly and the sun will not surprise you.

1. The whole rig in one picture

Every solar rig ever built is the same five things in the same order. Memorize the order and half of all troubleshooting is already done:

  • The panel turns light into DC power. It makes power only while light hits it, and it cannot store a single drop.
  • The charge controller sits between panel and battery like the keeper of a tap. It feeds the battery what the battery can take, and — just as important — stops when the battery is full. Panel straight into battery with no controller is how batteries die.
  • The battery is the bucket. Everything you use at night, on a cloudy day, or all at once comes out of the bucket. Panels refill it; loads drain it.
  • The inverter turns the battery's DC into wall-socket AC for things that demand a plug. It is a translator, and translators charge a fee — 10–20% of everything that passes through, plus a sip just for being awake.
  • The loads are your stuff: lights, phones, radios, a fridge. Every load has a watts number. That number runs this whole book.

The order never changes. Nothing skips the controller. Nothing but loads hangs off the inverter. When a rig misbehaves, walk the chain in order and you will find the sick link.

2. The words: every term on one page

Solar runs on about twenty words. Most of them are printed on labels and manuals as if you were born knowing them. You were not. Here they are, in the order you will meet them, in plain language. The water picture from the electricity book still works: volts are pressure, amps are flow, watts are how much work the water is doing.

Three formulas run the whole book: Watts = volts × amps. Watt-hours = watts × hours. Amp-hours × volts = watt-hours. Everything else is bookkeeping.

3. Reading a panel label: the sticker tells the truth

Every panel has a sticker on its back, and that sticker is the panel's whole confession. It looks like alphabet soup. It is actually five numbers that decide everything — what controller it can marry, how it can be chained, and what it will really do for you. Here is a typical 100W panel, decoded:

  • Pmax (100W) — the headline. True only in the lab. Plan on less (Entry 4) and you will never be disappointed.
  • Vmp × Imp = Pmax. 18.0 × 5.6 ≈ 100W. This is where the panel lives while it works. Notice the "12V panel" works at 18V — that extra pressure is what pushes charge into a 12V battery, and what MPPT controllers harvest (Entry 9).
  • Voc (22.5V) — the panel's max pressure, first thing on a cold morning with nothing plugged in. This is the number your controller's max input voltage must beat — with room to spare, because cold panels push Voc higher. Entry 7.
  • Isc (5.9A) — the panel's max flow. Controllers and fuses are sized from Isc, with a 25% safety margin on top.
  • Max system voltage (600V) — the highest chain voltage this panel's insulation is rated for. You will never get near it in a small rig, but it is a hard ceiling, not a suggestion.
  • Max fuse (10A) — if you parallel three or more strings of panels, each string gets a fuse no bigger than this. Entry 7.

Found a panel with no label? You can still interrogate it with a multimeter on a sunny day: volts across its two leads, nothing connected, is its Voc. Amps with the meter bridging the leads (meter set to the 10A DC range) is its Isc. Multiply Voc × Isc × 0.75 and you have a fair guess at its real working watts. Write the numbers on the frame in marker. Future-you says thanks.

4. Input: what your panels will really make

A 100W panel does not make 100W all day. It makes 100W at high noon on a perfect day in a lab. Out here, four honest facts shrink the harvest:

  • The sun is only "full" for a few hours. Mornings, evenings, and winter are slanted light. We count the day in peak sun hours: add up all the sunshine and squeeze it into equivalent hours of perfect noon. Rough guide for mid-latitudes: summer 5–6, spring/autumn 3.5–4.5, winter 1.5–2.5. Farther north, winter is worse; the tropics barely change.
  • Heat steals. A panel cooking on a summer roof can lose 10–20% just from being hot. Cool, bright days are secretly the best days.
  • Angle and dirt steal. A panel lying flat in winter, or wearing a coat of dust, gives up another chunk. Entry 15 fixes the angle; a wet rag fixes the dirt.
  • The chain takes a cut. Wire losses, the controller, the battery's own charging appetite — together figure on losing about a quarter of what the panel made before it lands in the bucket as usable energy.

The honest harvest formula: Daily Wh = panel watts × peak sun hours × 0.75. A 100W panel on a 5-hour summer day: 100 × 5 × 0.75 = 375Wh. That — not 100, and not 2,400 — is what you have to spend.

PanelGood day (5 peak hrs)Grey day (2 peak hrs)
50W≈190Wh≈75Wh
100W≈375Wh≈150Wh
200W≈750Wh≈300Wh
400W≈1,500Wh≈600Wh

Plan your life on the grey-day column and treat the good-day column as a bonus. A rig sized for the worst week of winter is a rig that never lets you down in July.

5. Output: what your stuff eats

Now the other side of the ledger. Every load eats watts × hours = watt-hours. Walk your shelter with a notebook and make the list — the label on the back of every device tells you its watts (or its volts and amps: multiply them).

LoadWattsTypical useWh per day
LED light5W5 hrs at night25Wh
Phone charge10–15Wabout 1.5 hrs≈15Wh
Laptop45–65W3 hrs≈150–180Wh
Handheld radio, charging5–10W2 hrs≈15Wh

Small fridge60–100W, but cyclesruns ≈⅓ of the day≈500–700Wh
Wi-Fi router (if anything's left to route)10W24 hrs240Wh
  • The fridge trap: a fridge's watts are what it eats while the motor runs. Motors cycle — roughly a third of the day for a healthy fridge in a cool room, half or more in a hot one. Budget with the duty cycle, not the label alone.
  • Always-on loads are silent budget killers. A 10W anything left on 24 hours eats 240Wh — more than your lights and phones combined. Hunt vampires: things glowing, warm, or humming that nobody is using.
  • Heat is the most expensive load there is. Anything that makes heat from electricity — kettle, space heater, hair dryer — eats 1,000W+. On a small rig, heat comes from fire, not batteries. (See Fire, Explained and Staying Warm and Keeping Cool, Explained.)

Your daily number: add the Wh column for everything you truly need. That total — call it your daily spend — is the number the whole rig gets sized around in the next entry.

6. The master math: sizing a rig end to end

Here is the whole design process, start to finish, on one worked example. Say your daily spend from Entry 5 is 1,000Wh — lights, phones, a laptop, a radio, a fan. Watch each piece fall out of the one before it:

  1. Panels: daily spend ÷ (peak sun hours × 0.75). Design for a middling day, 4 peak hours: 1,000 ÷ (4 × 0.75) = 333W of panel. Round up: 350–400W. More panel is the cheapest insurance there is — it is also your cloudy-day recovery.
  2. Battery: you want at least one full sunless day in the bucket, and you may only drink half a lead bucket. Usable Wh needed = 1,000Wh. Lead: 1,000 ÷ 0.5 = 2,000Wh of battery = at 12V, about 170Ah — call it two 100Ah batteries. Lithium: 1,000 ÷ 0.8 = 1,250Wh ≈ one 100Ah lithium.
  3. Controller: panel watts ÷ battery volts = charging amps. 400 ÷ 12 ≈ 33A, so a 40A controller — and its max input voltage must beat your panel chain's Voc with 25% headroom (Entry 7 does this math).
  4. Inverter: add up the watts of everything that might run at the same moment through plugs — say laptop 65W + fridge 100W + a light 10W = 175W — then add half again for surge and mercy: a 300–500W pure sine inverter. Bigger is not better: an oversized inverter sips more just being awake (Entry 13).

Check the balance: on the middling day, 400W of panel harvests 400 × 4 × 0.75 = 1,200Wh against a 1,000Wh spend. Surplus on average days, deficit on grey ones, and the bucket bridges the gap. If the bucket keeps shrinking week over week, the honest fixes are only ever the same two: more panel, or less spend.

That is the entire engineering of a small solar rig. Everything after this entry is the details of each link — how to chain them, what the limits are, and how long things take.

7. Chaining panels: series, parallel, and the maxes

One panel is a sip. Real rigs chain panels together, and there are exactly two ways to chain — the same two from the electricity book, now with panels:

  • Series: plus of one to minus of the next, like train cars. The volts add (18 + 18 = 36V), the amps stay at one panel's amps. Total watts are the same either way — what changes is the shape of the power. High volts travel down long wire with less loss, and MPPT controllers love them.
  • Parallel: all pluses together, all minuses together. The amps add (5.6 + 5.6 = 11.2A), the volts stay. Fatter flow needs fatter wire — and a shaded panel drags less on its neighbors.
  • Series-parallel: chains of series pairs, paralleled — how big rigs get both voltage and amps up. The math is just the two rules applied twice.

THE MAX THAT MATTERS — controller input volts: Add up the Voc of every panel in a series chain. That total must stay under the controller's max input voltage — with 25% headroom, because a cold bright morning raises Voc by roughly that much. Two panels at Voc 22.5V = 45V on paper, ~56V on a frosty dawn. Fine on a 100V controller. Dead 60V controller. Exceeding this max doesn't blow a fuse — it kills the controller, quietly and permanently.

  • The other max — amps: add the Isc of parallel chains, multiply by 1.25, and keep it under the controller's amp rating and your wire's comfort.
  • Chain twins. Panels in a series chain should be the same panel — same watts, same Voc. A chain is only as strong as its weakest panel: one small or shaded panel throttles the whole string. Parallel is more forgiving, but keep voltages alike there too.
  • Three or more parallel strings? Each string gets its own fuse, sized at or under the panel label's max fuse rating. With one or two strings a fault has nowhere dangerous to come from; with three, the healthy strings can gang up on the sick one.
  • Panels are live in light. There is no off switch on the sun. Cover a panel with a blanket or flip it face-down before you work on its wiring — that is its off switch.

8. Chaining batteries: building a bigger bucket

Same two chains, applied to the bucket. The electricity book's law still holds: twins only — same type, same size, same age, same charge before you chain them. A weak twin drags the strong one down to its level, permanently.

  • Series batteries: 12V + 12V = a 24V bank. Everything downstream — controller setting, inverter — must be the bank's voltage. Higher voltage moves the same watts with half the amps, which means thinner wire and less drop. This is why big rigs run 24V or 48V.
  • Parallel batteries: the bank stays 12V, the amp-hours add. Take the main leads off opposite ends of the bank — plus from one end battery, minus from the other — so every battery shares the work evenly instead of the nearest one doing it all.
  • The energy is identical either way: Ah × V = Wh. 24V × 100Ah = 12V × 200Ah = 2,400Wh. Chaining changes the shape, never the size of the bucket.
  • Never chain different voltages (a 12V and a 6V "to make 18") and never chain a fresh battery to a tired one. The bank becomes the worst battery in it.

9. The controller: PWM, MPPT, and what the letters buy you

The controller's job sounds simple — feed the battery, stop when full — but there are two kinds, and the difference is real money in harvested sunlight.

PWM (the simple tap). A PWM controller connects the panel to the battery in fast on-off pulses. The battery drags the panel's voltage down to its own — about 13–14V while charging. Remember the panel wants to work at its Vmp of 18V? On PWM, those spare volts are simply thrown away. Your 100W panel (18V × 5.6A) charges like a 75W panel (13.5V × 5.6A). Honest, cheap, rugged — and wasteful.

MPPT (the smart trader). An MPPT controller is a converter: it lets the panel work up at its happy 18V, then trades the spare volts for extra amps on the battery side — 100W in becomes roughly 100W out, delivered as 13.5V × 7.4A instead of 5.6A. Same panel, up to 25–30% more charge in the bucket, and even more on cold bright days when panel voltage runs high. It also unlocks series chaining: panels at 36V or 72V feeding a 12V battery, which PWM cannot do at all.

Sizing a controller — two checks: (1) Amps: panel watts ÷ battery volts, then add a quarter. 400W ÷ 12V = 33A → a 40A controller. (2) Volts: the series chain's total Voc × 1.25 for cold mornings must stay under its max input voltage (Entry 7). Pass both checks or pick a bigger controller.

  • Set the battery type. Every controller has a setting — flooded, sealed/AGM, lithium — and each charges to different voltages. Wrong setting = a battery slowly cooked or chronically underfed. Set it once, on purpose.
  • The connection order is sacred (from the electricity book): controller to battery first, panel last. Off in reverse: panel first, battery last. Many controllers sense the battery voltage to know what they are; feed them panel power with no battery and some never recover.
  • Charge stages, in plain words: Bulk — everything the panel's got, bucket's thirsty. Absorb — pressure held high while the flow tapers, topping the bucket gently. Float — full; a small maintenance sip to stay full. A battery that "stops charging at 80%" is usually just in absorb. Patience is a stage of charging.

10. Batteries: the honest numbers

The label on a battery is its best day, not its life. What you can actually use, night after night, depends on the chemistry:

TypeSafe to use (DoD)100Ah at 12V really givesPersonality
Flooded lead-acid (car, golf cart, marine)50%≈600WhCheap, heavy, everywhere. Needs water top-ups and air while charging. The scavenge king.
AGM / sealed lead-acid50%≈600WhNo watering, no spills, costs more, hates being left flat.
Lithium (LiFePO4)80–90%≈1,000WhHalf the weight, triple the life, most of the bucket is drinkable. Costs the most and is worth it if you find one. Needs the lithium controller setting.
  • Usable Wh = Ah × V × DoD. This is the only battery number that matters at 2 a.m. Write it on the battery in marker.
  • Lead batteries die of thirst and neglect, lithium of abuse. Lead wants to be refilled promptly and never left sitting flat — a lead battery left discharged for weeks sulfates and never comes back. Charge it the day you drain it.
  • Cold shrinks every bucket. A lead battery near freezing may give only 60–70% of its rated self. Keep batteries in the warmest spot you can — inside the living space's insulation line if possible, off bare concrete, in a vented box if they're flooded.
  • Car starter batteries are sprinters. Built for one huge gulp, not deep sips. They'll work — everything works at first — but deep-cycling kills them in months. Deep-cycle, marine, golf cart, and wheelchair batteries are the marathoners. Scavenge accordingly (Entry 16).

11. How long does it take to charge?

The question everyone asks, with an honest answer: it depends on three numbers, and you know all of them.

Charge time: Hours of good sun = Wh you need to replace ÷ (panel watts × 0.75). And a day only holds 4–6 peak sun hours — so "10 hours of charging" usually means two days.

Worked examples, using a 100W panel at the honest 75W:

ChargingWh to replaceGood sun neededReal answer
A phone (15Wh battery)≈20Wh with losses≈15 minutesOne coffee break at noon
A laptop≈60Wh≈1 hourAn afternoon
A power tool battery≈100Wh≈1.5 hoursSame day, easily
100Ah lead battery, from half (its safe floor)600Wh8 hoursAbout two decent days
The same battery on a 400W rig600Wh2 hoursOne good morning
  • Charging slows at the top. The last 20% (the absorb stage, Entry 9) trickles in slowly no matter how big the panel is. A battery at 80% by noon is a healthy rig, not a broken one.
  • Using power while charging splits the stream. If the panel makes 75W and the laptop takes 60W, the battery gets the leftover 15W. Charge times assume the loads are off — or subtract them first.
  • Small things charge straight off panels through a USB solar charger or the controller's USB port — but never plug a bare panel straight into a phone. Panel voltage swings with every cloud; the controller (or the charger built into a folding USB panel) is what keeps it polite.
  • Winter doubles everything. Half the peak hours, half the harvest, twice the wait. Plan winter life around the grey-day column from Entry 4.

12. How long will it run?

The mirror question, and the easier one:

Runtime: Hours = usable Wh in the bucket ÷ the load's watts. Usable Wh is the honest number from Entry 10 — Ah × volts × depth of discharge.

One 100Ah lead battery at 12V — 600 usable Wh — running common loads, one at a time:

LoadWattsRuns for
LED light5W120 hours — five full days of nights
Phone charging15W40 charges' worth of hours — weeks of phones
12V fan15W40 hours
Laptop60W10 hours
CPAP40W15 hours — about two nights. Plan the rig around this one if it's yours.
Small fridge (⅓ duty cycle)≈30W average≈20 hours — one sunless day, no more
  • Loads add. Light + fan + laptop together = 80W = 7.5 hours. The bucket doesn't care what drinks from it; it only counts watts.
  • Through an inverter, shave 15% off every runtime — the translator's fee. The same light on DC runs 120 hours; through an inverter, about 100. This is why smart rigs run everything they can on 12V and USB, straight off the battery.
  • The inverter itself drinks. An inverter left switched on with nothing plugged in still sips 5–15W, all night, every night. Off means off. A big inverter idling can eat more than your lights.
  • When the bucket runs low, shed in order: comfort first (fan), then convenience (laptop), and protect the critical ones (light, radio, CPAP, fridge) to the end. Decide the order now, in daylight, not at 2 a.m.

13. Inverters: the last box in the chain

The inverter turns the battery's steady DC into wall-style AC. Three numbers and one shape decide if it's the right one:

  • Continuous watts — what it can deliver all day. Size it from Entry 6: everything that might run at once, plus half again.
  • Surge watts — usually double the continuous, for a few seconds. Motors (fridge, pump, power tools) gulp 3–5× their running watts for a heartbeat at startup. If the surge can't cover the gulp, the inverter trips and the motor never starts — even though the running watts looked fine on paper.
  • Input voltage — the inverter must match the bank: a 12V inverter on a 24V bank is a brief, expensive firework. Check before you connect.
  • Pure sine vs modified sine — pure sine is identical to wall power and everything loves it. Modified sine is a choppy imitation: fine for phone chargers, lights, and most simple tools; risky for variable-speed motors, some laptop bricks (they buzz and run hot), medical gear, and anything with a timer or audio you care about. If it hums, buzzes, or runs hot on modified sine, believe it and stop.

The DC-first rule: every trip through the inverter costs 10–20%. Lights, fans, phones, radios, even many TVs exist in 12V or USB versions. Plug-shaped loads are the last resort, not the default. The best inverter is the one that's usually off.

14. Wire and fuses: the long road from the roof

The electricity book's two thieves — thin wire and long distance — rob solar rigs worst of all, because the panel usually lives far from the battery and low-voltage power hates to travel. The rules that keep the harvest from leaking away on the road:

  • Raise the volts for the journey. This is series chaining's superpower (Entry 7): 200W at 36V loses a third as much in the same wire as 200W at 12V. Panel run long? Chain in series, let an MPPT sort it out at the battery end.
  • Keep the battery end short and fat. The controller-to-battery and battery-to-inverter runs carry the biggest amps in the system. Short, thick cable — the fattest you have — and no exceptions.
  • Fuse at the battery, on the plus wire, sized to the wire and the load — same law as the electricity book. Add a disconnect or a breaker on the panel line too, so "panel off" is a switch, not a blanket (though the blanket works).
  • Warm wire is a warning. Any run you can feel getting warm is a run that's stealing power and auditioning to be a fire. Thicker wire or higher volts. Fix it the day you notice it.
  • Outdoor runs need outdoor wire. Panel cable is UV-proof for a reason. Indoor lamp cord on a roof is insulation on a countdown.

15. Pointing panels: free watts for zero parts

Before you buy another panel, aim the ones you have. A well-aimed 100W panel can out-harvest a flat 150W panel all winter.

  • Face the equator. Northern hemisphere: face them south. Southern: north. A compass app or the noon shadow tells you true enough — the shadow at solar noon points straight away from the equator.
  • Tilt = your latitude. The set-and-forget angle: tilt the panel from flat by roughly your latitude in degrees (30–40° for most of the US). Winter matters more than summer? Add 15°. Summer only? Subtract 15°. Flat on a roof is fine in summer and sad in winter.
  • Shade is not proportional — it's brutal. Panels are chains of cells, and a chain sags to its weakest link: shade on one corner can cut a panel's output in half or worse, and in a series chain it throttles every panel chained with it. One chimney shadow at 3 p.m. is a design problem, not bad luck. Watch the panel's patch of sky for a full day before you bolt anything down.
  • Clean them. Dust, pollen, and bird opinions cost 5–25%. Water and a soft rag, morning or evening — never cold water on hot glass at noon.
  • Snow slides off tilted panels and blankets flat ones. In snow country, steep is self-cleaning.
  • Air underneath. A panel with a gap behind it runs cooler than one hugging a roof — and cooler is more watts (Entry 4). Even a couple of inches of airflow pays.

16. What to scavenge for solar

The pre-collapse world scattered solar gear everywhere, and most people walk right past it because it doesn't look like food. It is better than food — it's the factory that charges everything else:

  • Solar panels — rooftops, RVs, boats, garden sheds, dead solar farms, highway signs, parking-lot lights. A panel with intact glass and no burn marks is almost certainly fine — panels outlive everything attached to them. Test with a multimeter (Entry 3) and write its numbers on the frame.
  • Charge controllers — wherever panels were: RVs, cabins, boats, sheds. Check the label for its two maxes (amps out, volts in) before you marry it to panels. An MPPT unit is a genuine prize; a PWM is still a working tap.
  • Deep-cycle batteries — golf carts, wheelchairs and mobility scooters, floor scrubbers, boat houses, UPS battery cabinets in offices (small ones, but many). Deep-cycle beats car batteries every time (Entry 10). Test before you haul: a battery that won't hold 12V overnight won't hold anything.
  • Inverters — car accessories, truck cabs, RVs. Check the input voltage matches your bank, and prefer pure sine. A modified-sine unit still runs chargers and lights fine.
  • Garden solar lights — every yard and cemetery path. Each one is a tiny panel, a small rechargeable cell, and an LED in a free weatherproof case. Useless alone, wonderful by the dozen: path markers, night lights, and the cells recharge AA batteries in a pinch.
  • Power banks and folding USB panels — camping kits, emergency kits, glove boxes. Small buckets, but they charge the things you actually carry. A folding panel with a built-in regulator is a phone's best friend.
  • Cable and connectors — panel leads are usually MC4-style click connectors; take the pigtails with the panel. Fat battery cable lives in cars and boats. Take fuses and holders from every dead car's fuse box you pass.
  • A multimeter — the one tool this whole book leans on. Toolboxes, electricians' vans, school labs. Volts and amps, measured, end every argument about what a panel or battery really is.

Makes: a two-battery 12V bank, 1,200 usable Wh, boxed and fused — the heart of a stay-put rig.

19. Stop signs

  • A series chain whose Voc × 1.25 is anywhere near the controller's max input voltage. Do the cold-morning math before you plug in, not after the smell.
  • Working on panel wiring in full sun without covering the panel first. A panel in light is live. The blanket is the off switch.
  • A battery bank of mixed types, ages, sizes, or voltages. Twins only — "just to try it" is how banks die.
  • A battery that is swollen, cracked, leaking, or hissing, or a rotten-egg smell near a charging battery. Walk away, air it out, no sparks. (Same law as the electricity book — it does not expire.)
  • A panel with shattered glass in a high-voltage chain. Small rigs forgive a lot; a big string's voltage through cracked, wet glass is not small-rig forgiveness.
  • Any load that buzzes, hums, or runs hot on a modified-sine inverter. The load is telling you the truth. Believe it and unplug it.
  • Any wire too hot to hold, any connector too warm to be innocent. Fix it today.
  • Anyone who wants to backfeed this rig into a house's breaker panel to "power the house." That is the other kind of electricity, the killing kind, and it is not in this book.
  • The moment you think "I'm not sure." That thought is the skill working. Listen to it.

Knowing where your skill ends is most of having the skill.

20. The whole book on one card

  • The chain never changes: panel → controller → battery → inverter → loads.
  • Watts = volts × amps. Watt-hours = watts × hours. Amp-hours × volts = watt-hours.
  • Honest harvest: panel watts × peak sun hours × 0.75. Plan on the grey day.
  • Runtime: usable Wh ÷ load watts. Usable = Ah × V × DoD — lead gives half, lithium gives four-fifths.
  • Series adds volts, parallel adds amps. The watts stay the same; only the shape changes.
  • Controller maxes are law: chain Voc × 1.25 under its max input volts; panel watts ÷ battery volts × 1.25 under its amps.
  • MPPT trades spare volts for extra amps — up to a third more harvest. PWM just throws the volts away.
  • Controller to battery first, panel last. Off in reverse. Cover the panel to turn it off.
  • DC first — the inverter is a tax plus rent. Off means off.
  • Not sure = stop.
